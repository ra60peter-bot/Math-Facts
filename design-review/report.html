1. Product and users

This is an automaticity practice tool, not a complete mathematics curriculum. The learner generally knows the facts and is developing faster, more reliable recall.

The application covers multiplication with factors 2–12, addition using numbers 1–9, and positive subtraction facts using numbers 1–10. Each ordered fact is tracked independently: 9 × 7 and 7 × 9 can have different progress. Students choose an operation, a question count, and individual facts or groups. Selected facts remain available for unrestricted practice; the scheduler prefers useful assessment, training, and later checks without preventing extra practice.

The existing speed goal is a correct, unassisted response beginning within approximately 1.5 seconds of question presentation. Practice results feed saved history and an adaptive scheduler. Durable mastery requires later, unprimed checks across days; repeating a visible fact is useful practice but does not provide the same evidence. The operation-wide 0–1,000 performance score and verified mastery count describe different things.

An adult accepts an invitation, sets a password, and adds student profiles. On a recognized family device, a child selects their profile without a password. Owner controls require authentication; student access is limited to practice and their own records. This distinction is worth retaining. The child should independently choose practice, understand readiness, answer, recover, stop, and inspect understandable progress. Invitations, account recovery, student deletion, and detailed technical diagnostics belong with the adult.

Scope and method. The review combines repository inspection, research, a local replay of the actual React interface using fictional data, and a separate interactive proposal. The live production inspection was limited to the owner sign-in surface. Authenticated child journeys are examined through code and the local replay, not claimed as a live production account audit. No real students, audio, or production records were used. Browser verification is recorded in section 9.

2. What the evidence supports

Clear interfaces support independence

Design guidanceNielsen’s usability heuristics support visible system status, familiar language, recognition rather than memory, and actionable recovery. Here, those principles mean a child should not have to infer whether a transcript is provisional, whether an answer counted, or whether the microphone is ready. They are inspection principles, not measured learning benefits.

Observational researchNN/g’s teen research involved 100 participants aged 13–17 across three rounds. Technical familiarity did not guarantee successful navigation; age-appropriate presentation and concise content mattered. Only ages 13–14 directly overlap this audience. A restrained design is a sound starting point, but preferences of ages 10–12 must be tested rather than assumed. “Child-friendly” does not require childish graphics.

Retrieval and spacing are stronger foundations than decoration

Primary researchRoediger and colleagues’ sixth-grade classroom experiments found better later retention after low-stakes quizzes than after unquizzed or repeated exposure. The age fit is good; the social-studies material and test outcomes differ from spoken arithmetic. This supports retrieving answers and returning later, not a particular threshold or exact scheduler interval.

Schutte and colleagues compared equal amounts of timed addition practice among 48 third graders over 19 days. Distributing four minutes into multiple periods improved fluency growth more than consecutive practice. The learners were younger and responses were written. Recommend manageable rounds and return visits, while keeping unlimited voluntary practice. Do not imply that the exact proposed round length has been experimentally optimized.

Timing has a purpose, but pressure needs care

Evidence-based guidanceThe 2021 IES mathematics intervention guide recommends timed activities, usually 1–5 minutes, after concepts have been learned. They are one component of instruction, not the whole intervention. This supports the product’s recall goal but does not validate 1.5 seconds from visual presentation to speech onset as a universal definition of mastery.

Primary researchPassolunghi and colleagues compared 66 students with high or low mathematics anxiety, drawn from ages 11–13. Higher anxiety was associated with weaker mathematics and working-memory performance. This cross-sectional study cannot show that a visible timer causes anxiety. The balanced implication is to preserve measured fluency while testing a calm presentation: no running countdown, correctness acknowledged first, and speed described as a skill being developed.

Useful feedback is specific and trustworthy

Fyfe and Rittle-Johnson’s computerized mathematics study found benefits from immediate feedback after prior instruction, with effects depending on prior knowledge. Its second-grade population and equivalence problems limit direct transfer. It supports informative correction, not flashing labels or arbitrary feedback delays. Mueller and Dweck’s fifth-grade experiments also favor care with praise: intelligence praise led to less persistence after failure than effort praise. Use truthful observations about progress instead of fixed-ability labels.

Enjoyment and learning are not interchangeable

Plass and colleagues’ randomized arithmetic-game study with 58 middle-school students found motivational differences between individual, competitive, and collaborative play, but no between-condition advantage in out-of-game fluency. Sailer and Homner’s meta-analysis found positive average gamification effects with substantial variation; some motivational and behavioral findings were less stable in stronger studies. Neither establishes that eight seconds of music and animation improves this application’s learning outcomes. Retain optional acknowledgment; avoid requiring a reward sequence before reviewing results.

Speech errors need a route forward

Guidance and primary researchGoogle’s conversation-design guidance distinguishes no input, no match, and system errors. A five-week family voice-assistant study observed children repeating, reformulating, and abandoning requests when recovery was unclear. Its 11 children were ages 6–11, only partly overlapping this audience, and Alexa is not a number recognizer. The relevant design hypothesis is concrete recovery: “That answer wasn’t clear. Try again or type it. This attempt wasn’t scored.”

3. Audit of the complete journey

The following findings are tied to current components and replayable states. Labels distinguish code evidence, observed local behavior, and design judgments. The current-interface screenshots below were captured in a Chromium in-app browser on Windows at 1280 × 720, using the real React interface in fictional local owner mode with simulated recognition. They do not establish real microphone performance or reproduce a production student account. Section 9 records the broader verification limits.

First visit and identity: preserve the role split

Code evidenceweb/components/profile-gate.tsx provides a student picker on an owner-connected device and a separate owner-password path. AppFrame shows identity prominently and suppresses management navigation for students. This supports a child returning independently without exposing adult controls.

The invitation/password/setup journey still needs testing with a fresh adult account. The proposal names the adult area “Family controls,” explains why the first visit needs an adult, and returns the device to a clear person picker. A fictional password dialog demonstrates this distinction; it is not a replacement authentication implementation.

Practice selection: put the learning choice before technical choices

Code evidenceweb/components/math-facts-app.tsx places a large voice-engine section before session settings and the always-visible fact grid. It can discuss a 40 MB model, browser recognition, an on-device pack, and fallback states. The engine details are accurate operational information, but compete with the child’s primary decision: what to practice.

Observed locallyIn the owner-mode replay, the voice panel occupied substantial first-screen space while the fact grid began below the initial viewport. Adult-only diagnostics make that replay longer than student mode; code inspection confirms the prominent engine section and controls remain in student setup.

Design judgmentLead with operation, round length, answer method, and fact choice. Keep the complete grid visible; top headings remain the first number. Put download and microphone status into preparation, with technical detail behind help. Preserve All/Clear and individual/group selection. Empty selection needs a nearby explanation rather than an unexplained disabled start button. A default short round is a starting suggestion, never a limit.

Starting: distinguish an engine being ready from a microphone working

Code evidenceThe local model prepares on load, but “Number recognition active” identifies an engine mode. It does not itself prove that permission, capture, and recognition of this child’s sample answer succeed. The production speech test is hidden from student mode.

Provide one ordinary-number check before the first round or after an input failure. State what happens next before requesting permission. A ready state should mean a sample has been recognized, with a clear Start action. On later visits, remember successful setup where appropriate without pretending a revoked permission or unplugged microphone is still working.

Active practice: keep the central fact, bring status closer

Code evidenceThe practice branch in web/components/math-facts-app.tsx, styled by web/app/globals.css, has a large central fact, completion count, explicit exit, central feedback, and microphone/transcript controls at the bottom. Its responsive font sizing and restrained palette are strengths. Question reveal waits for audio readiness, and the feedback region already uses a polite live announcement.

The footer can separate the input explanation from the fact and result. Group Listening, Checking, and recovery beneath the question so the learner follows one attention path. Keep Pause in the top toolbar and End consistently available in the footer. Preserve the transcript as evidence, but do not let “Heard: 28” imply that an uncertain hypothesis has been fully scored. Use one coherent state at a time.

Correct and incorrect responses: retain accuracy, clarify the next action

Code evidenceCurrent feedback already distinguishes “Correct! Within target,” “Correct; needs speed practice,” and “Incorrect.” Fast/slow correct responses advance after approximately 950/1,450 ms; wrong responses wait for a decision and show the correct answer. This is better than treating slow-but-correct as a mathematical failure.

Observed locallyThe replay advanced through correct answers. A simulated answer of 16 for 3 × 5 produced “Incorrect · 0.8 seconds,” showed 15 centrally, and offered correction acknowledgment in the footer. The interaction was functional; its explanation and response controls were spatially separated from the feedback.

The proposed “Correct — building speed” shortens the message. Keep response time available without forcing a live countdown. Offer an auto-advance preference because reading speeds and assistive-technology needs vary. After a wrong answer, use explicit actions such as “Next question” and “The app heard me wrong.” The existing “Acknowledge correction” wording is abstract; it should not be necessary to understand scoring terminology before continuing.

Recognition failure: unblock the learner without inventing knowledge

Code evidenceThe implementation contains unscored invalid-attempt paths and useful diagnostic traces. However, ordinary recovery asks the learner to tap Mic or change recognition mode in setup; there is no typed-answer practice path. A permission failure can therefore prevent independent use even when mathematics knowledge is intact.

Observed locallyFor a simulated transcript containing words but no number, the center still said “Say your answer aloud” while the footer explained that no number was recognized, no answer was scored, and Mic should be tapped. Keeping the attempt unscored is correct; the competing central instruction makes the current state less coherent.

Offer Try again and Type instead in the same place as the error. After repeated failures, make the alternative prominent. Currently, acknowledgment updates the message and advances; it does not persist a distinct disputed-recognition record. The proposal needs that durable distinction, not a silent conversion into unassisted success. Once the answer has been revealed, a retry cannot establish fresh recall. Keep diagnostic logs available for an adult without making them the child’s recovery task.

Pause and early exit: make stopping ordinary

Code evidenceExit practice records partial usage, and unfinished sessions have a resume flow. The local replay was exited partway through a round and displayed its saved partial results. There is no explicit pause control in the main practice toolbar. Add Pause with Resume and End & save. Stop capture and timing while paused. If a fact has already been seen, resume it as practice rather than a new cold check. Explain this gently; do not make a child feel penalized for taking a break.

Results and return visits: explain the record

Code evidenceResults show accuracy, questions and average response time; Repeat keeps configuration. History exposes individual answers, heard transcripts, and result sorting. These are valuable transparency features. The generic “Session complete” heading can also appear after early exit; use “Round saved” when incomplete.

Session averages include all recorded responses, while report averages use correct responses; that difference needs explicit labels. Neither prominently explains timing quality. Show the number of comparable spoken first attempts, separating retries, typed answers and unavailable timing. A concise result summary should lead to Repeat, Choose other facts, and My progress. Existing celebrations already have mute/dismiss controls and reduced-motion CSS; improve preference persistence and age fit rather than removing successful safeguards.

Progress: distinguish recent performance from remembered mastery

Code evidenceweb/components/mastery-progress.tsx labels an operation-wide score; web/components/history-progress.tsx separately reports mastered, verifying, slow, needs-practice and untried facts. The report includes a long encouraging narrative and all-fact table. This provides detail, but makes “What should I practice?” require interpretation and scrolling.

Offer a short child summary, a visible mastered count, and filterable facts with a direct Practice action. Explain the 0–1,000 score as latest accuracy and speed across every fact in that operation; selected subsets never redefine the denominator. Label remembered mastery separately. Keep the richer narrative and diagnostics available for adults. Preserve the existing disclosure that deleting history does not reset stored mastery or scheduling.

4. Make speech timing fair and understandable

The Web Speech API draft specification distinguishes audio capture, sound, speech, recognition results, and no-match events. Noise is not necessarily speech; an interim transcript is not final. Implementations may differ in detection and processing. The specification is not a measurement guarantee for Chrome, Safari, or the local recognizer.

1. Recall responseQuestion visible → speech onset
2. SpeakingSpeech onset → utterance ends
3. RecognitionAudio processing → usable number
4. InterfaceUsable number → feedback painted

The product intends to measure the first interval. A learner can start “twenty-seven” quickly even if finishing the word or obtaining a transcript takes longer. Keep Checking separate from timed recall. Record timing provenance and uncertainty rather than claiming two-decimal display precision proves two-decimal accuracy. Keyboard completion time includes motor input and cannot silently substitute for spoken-onset time.

The recognition handlers in web/components/math-facts-app.tsx use engine word timing when available, browser speech events otherwise, with an estimated path when direct onset is unavailable. That estimate can appear in a transient message, but timing provenance is not retained as a separate field in the saved attempt. Validate these paths against synchronized audio and screen recordings before changing thresholds. Background noise, words begun during transitions, stalled capture, short syllables, repeated answers, and delayed final transcripts need dedicated cases. A redesign can expose uncertainty clearly; it cannot by itself repair an acoustic model.

The proposal uses a compact identity rail with Practice, My progress, History and Switch person. Adult management remains separate. The main desktop area pairs round choices with an always-available fact grid. A compact operation progress strip supports orientation without dominating setup. Typography is straightforward, with generous control targets, restrained green accents and a contrasting progress color.

During practice, one central question and status cluster guide attention. Microphone setup occurs before scoring. Correctness, speed and recognition uncertainty have distinct messages; actions remain stable. Results appear immediately and remain readable during optional celebrations. These are reasoned design choices, not claims of measured superiority. The working mockup makes them testable.

Accessibility is part of the direction

WCAG 2.2 supplies requirements, not a decorative checklist: keyboard operation, visible/unobscured focus, non-color status labels, adequate contrast and target size. Its AA target-size baseline is 24 CSS pixels with exceptions; the proposal’s larger controls are a usability choice. Test zoom, focus order, table navigation and screen-reader announcements across the complete journey.

Timing Adjustable permits an essential-timing exception in defined circumstances, not blanket exclusion of every user. Preserve timed fluency while offering untimed accuracy practice. Status Messages supports announcing meaningful states without moving focus; do not announce every timer tick. Pause, Stop, Hide and interaction-animation guidance support controllable nonessential motion. Full conformance is not established by this review.

10. Validate with children and adults separately

Usability: can a learner use it without rescue?

Recruit a small first round of approximately six to eight children spanning ages 10–14, varied reading confidence, math confidence and computer familiarity. Include relevant access needs with appropriate support. Obtain adult consent and child assent. Use ordinary laptops, including a smaller 1366 × 768-class window, a MacBook, keyboard-only use and zoom. Counterbalance current and proposed interfaces to reduce order effects; do not coach before a child attempts recovery.

Ask each learner to identify their profile, choose specified facts, start a round, explain when to speak, handle a deliberately unclear answer, switch input, pause, finish early, repeat and explain their progress. Record task success, adult interventions, recovery attempts, mistaken clicks, status comprehension, and a brief comfort/confidence rating. Ask what “correct but building speed,” “unscored,” and “mastered later” mean. Preference alone is insufficient: look for successful independent actions.

Parent tasks

Separately test invitation recognition, password creation, student creation, handoff, owner re-entry, interpretation of reports and deletion consequences. Verify that adults understand what children can access and that a history deletion does not reset mastery. Use a disposable test environment, not production families. Check expired links, missing emails, no students, forgotten passwords, offline saves and failed synchronization.

Learning and timing: different questions, different measures

Before comparing recall speed, validate onset measurements against synchronized screen/audio recordings across Chrome, Edge and Safari. Include short numbers, longer numbers, accents, varying volume, background noise and delayed recognizer output. Report onset error distributions and invalid-rate differences by engine; exclude uncertain timing rather than averaging it as fact knowledge.

For a later learning pilot, compare equivalent practice exposure and assess accuracy plus spoken latency on delayed, unprimed tests, including untrained facts where appropriate. Separate changes in enjoyment or time-on-task from changes in retention and automatic recall. Analyze comparable first attempts, document retries, and avoid using immediate repeated answers as independent mastery evidence. A small usability study cannot establish learning gains.

Open decisions to validate. Does the default round feel manageable? Do learners prefer automatic next questions? Can they distinguish the 0–1,000 performance score from mastered facts? How often does input recovery require an adult? Is a short optional celebration welcome across ages 10–14? Resolve these with observation before expanding the feature set.
